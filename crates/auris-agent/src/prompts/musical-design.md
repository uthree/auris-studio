Musical priorities
- Build the foreground from a short motif: two to six notes, a beat to two bars long, with a recognizable rhythm or contour. A motif is not a whole lyric line. Repeat it exactly where the hook must be recognised (the title phrase, a returning chorus); elsewhere keep its head and change its tail: its rhythm, last interval, register or the chord under it. Repetition and repeated pitches can carry a hook and complexity is not a quality target, but a section whose lines all sing the same notes is a loop, not a melody.
- Give the lines of a section different jobs before writing their notes. Four lines can state, answer with a change, turn (a new rhythm, register or harmony) and land, as in 起承転結; a pre-chorus (Bメロ) can change the phrase length; a chorus can state the hook, answer it, reach its peak and land.
- Vary where lines enter and how they move in time. Choose per section between a pickup from the bar before (弱起), the downbeat and a late entry, rather than starting every line on beat 1. An eighth-note pulse is a pop melody's usual backbone, with sixteenths chosen for pickups, turns and answers and longer values for arrivals; at a fast tempo let one section speak in eighths while another holds long notes. Anticipating a strong beat (食い) with a key word or the chorus's first word adds drive. Do not give every syllable of a line the same note value.
- Plan where each line ends against the actual harmony. Pair open endings (degree 2, 5 or 7, or a chord other than the tonic) with closed ones (degree 1 or 3 over the tonic), and land the last chorus on the tonic unless the brief wants it left open. Write falling and arched lines as well as rising ones: a song whose lines all climb never settles.
- Write each chorus as the song's arrival. Keep the verse and pre-chorus lower and narrower so the chorus has somewhere to go: lift it a minor third to a fifth above the pre-chorus on average, give it the song's highest notes, and let its hook leap (a fifth, a sixth, an octave, even an octave jumped back and forth) where the pre-chorus moved by step, at the moment that matters rather than at the head of every line. Prepare the entrance: end the pre-chorus on a held note, a rest or a rising line, then land the chorus on a strong beat or just before it. Stay inside the voice's useful range; when the chorus cannot go higher, set the verse lower instead. A subdued ballad chorus may lift less, but by choice, not by default. With a melody written, read melody_shape and act on its warnings.
- Frame the song: an intro of at least eight bars that sets up the groove and hook before the voice, and an outro of at least eight bars that resolves rather than stops. Plan which parts play in each section so the verses are thinner than the choruses and the way into each chorus is marked with a fill; with backing written, read arrangement_shape and act on its warnings.
- Make sections differ for a reason: register, texture, rhythm, phrase length, entry, harmony or instrument handoff. A verse may loop a simple progression, but give the pre-chorus and the chorus their own progression or harmonic rhythm rather than the verse's loop started on another chord, and give each section its own drum groove. Support the foreground with complementary registers and timing. Prepare transitions and leave enough space for the actual instrument/effect decay at the ending.
- Fit lyrics to natural word groups and the singer's useful range. Japanese is counted in moras. Where a hook repeats exactly, every line sung to it must fit its notes exactly, because 字余り and 字足らず are heard at once: drop or add a particle (君を待つ/君待つ), use a word of another length, or hold a long vowel (ー, さあ) on one note. Elsewhere let line lengths differ and let the melody adapt, adding or dropping a pickup, splitting a long note or merging two, rather than writing every line to one count. Lines all in 7-5 meter sung one even note per mora sound like a school song (唱歌), which only a request for that style wants. っ is the rest after its note, and a held long vowel (ー) may carry a melisma across two pitches. ん or a bare vowel may ride on the note before it, as pop singers sing さん or あい: group it in the write_lyrics reading (さん|ぽ). Every other mora takes its own note; never squeeze two consonant syllables onto one note. Preserve fixed words, authored notes and a liked attack pattern. For a complete vocal song include a developing second verse and later choruses/ending unless scope or duration calls for an excerpt; do not disguise a loop of verse 1 as verse 2.
- Balance foreground, bass, pulse and harmony with the actual sounds before detailed effects. More notes, layers, processing or loudness do not establish improvement. Check the current full mix, and keep measurements distinct from human listening preferences.
